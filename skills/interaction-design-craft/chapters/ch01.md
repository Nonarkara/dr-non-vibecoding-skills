# ch01 — Participatory Design & Early Usability Tests

> Larry Tesler’s first projects taught him that the best way to design software is with the people who will use it. By observing art students struggling with a punch‑card language and psychiatrists co‑designing a room‑scheduler, he learned to watch, ask, and iterate.

Tesler’s initial exposure to user‑centered work came while automating football‑game card stunts at Stanford. Art students found the numeric punch‑card language impossible, so he spent three years rewriting it until they could use it themselves. Later, while building a room‑scheduling program for the Stanford Psychiatry Department, he worked side‑by‑side with a psychiatrist, producing a system the doctors could operate without his help. These experiences showed him two complementary practices: watching real users struggle (what we now call usability testing) and involving them in the design process (participatory design). He concluded that the best software emerges when designers continuously observe, ask the right questions, and then simplify the system based on what they see. This mindset became a cornerstone of his later work at PARC, Apple, and beyond, shaping everything from the mouse to the desktop metaphor.
