# ch10 — Evolution of the Desktop Metaphor: Mouse Buttons, Affordances, and Feedback

> The desktop metaphor succeeded not because it copied a real desk, but because it gave users clear affordances—visual and tactile cues that signal how to interact—while evolving the mouse from one to multiple buttons as needed.

Early mouse experiments showed that a single button could be enough for novices, but experts wanted shortcuts. Microsoft’s 1987 mouse added a second button for context menus, preserving learnability while offering power. The desktop metaphor’s strength lies in its affordances: the screen’s top edge invites horizontal scanning, the blinking cursor signals where text will appear, the trash can’s emptiness indicates whether something is inside, and the highlighted menu item shows activation. Feedback is immediate: menus pop down on mouse‑over, buttons depress when clicked, and the cursor changes shape over resize handles. Designers should therefore look for ways to make the possible actions perceptible (affordance) and the results obvious (feedback), rather than relying on users to remember arbitrary mappings. This principle scales from the original desktop to touch screens, voice interfaces, and AR/VR, where the goal remains to let users recognize what to do next.
