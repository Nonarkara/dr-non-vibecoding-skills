# ch01 — The Sunk Cost Fallacy

> Explains why we irrationally persist in failing endeavors due to past investments. Provides a rule for making decisions based on future utility rather than past loss.

The sunk cost fallacy occurs when we allow past investments of time, money, or emotion to dictate our future actions, even when the current path is clearly unproductive. This happens because humans have an innate need for consistency and a fear of admitting error. For example, an investor might refuse to sell a plummeting stock because they 'already lost too much,' or a couple might stay in a toxic relationship because they have 'put so much work into it.' In both cases, the resources are gone regardless of the next move. To think clearly, you must ignore what has already been spent. Rationality requires looking only at future costs and benefits. If a project is no longer viable, the most logical step is to cut your losses immediately. The money spent yesterday is irrelevant to the decision you make today. Rule: Base every decision on the future outlook, never on the price of admission you already paid.
