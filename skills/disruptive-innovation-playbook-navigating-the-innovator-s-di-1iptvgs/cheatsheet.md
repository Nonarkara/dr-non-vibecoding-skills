# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## product

- If your biggest customers demand higher performance, ask whether a lower‑performance, lower‑price alternative could open a new market.
- If performance improvements are outpacing customer willingness to pay, start looking for price‑sensitive customers.
- When a disruptive technology appears, focus on the attributes valued by its early adopters, not on the attributes valued by your current customers.
- When a technology’s performance curve intersects the market demand curve, that is the moment to launch.

## general

- When a competitor enters at a lower price, assume they are right about the cost structure and you are wrong.
- Never let the voice of your most profitable customers drown out signals from emerging customers.

## process

- Never allocate capital based on a market forecast that you cannot verify.
- Match the size of the team to the current revenue potential of the market it serves.
- Treat every forecast as a hypothesis to be tested, not a fact.
- Monitor the gap between technology supply and market demand to predict shifts in competition.
- Use small, reversible bets to learn about a new market before committing large resources.
- When your cost structure is high‑margin, redesign processes to lower fixed costs before entering low‑margin markets.
- Track performance metrics separately for the disruptive unit to avoid contamination by legacy goals.
- If you cannot quantify the market, focus on learning the customer’s problem instead.

## organization

- Create a separate unit for any project that requires a different cost structure than your core business.
- If your organization’s values prioritize high margin, you cannot succeed in a low‑margin market without changing those values.
- Separate incentives for the new business from those of the legacy business.
- If your organization’s processes are rigid, create a new process team for the disruptive project.
- Allocate a dedicated budget for disruptive experiments; treat it as a sandbox, not a cost center.
- If your organization’s values reward speed over cost, you will struggle with low‑margin disruptive markets.

## design

- When a new architecture is technically simple, assume it is likely to be disruptive.
- When a new technology is simple and uses off‑the‑shelf parts, assume it is likely to be disruptive.

## strategy

- Avoid waiting for a market to become ‘big enough’; enter early with a minimal viable product.
- If incumbents are slow to adopt a new technology, look for entrants that can move faster.
- If a project threatens to cannibalize existing revenue, evaluate it on its own growth potential, not on short‑term loss.
