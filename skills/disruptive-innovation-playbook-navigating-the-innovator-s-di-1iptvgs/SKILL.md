## Core principles

- Great firms fail when they listen only to their most profitable customers and ignore emerging low‑margin markets.
- Disruptive technologies create new markets that cannot be forecast with traditional market‑size analysis.
- Large incumbents cannot allocate resources to small, fast‑growing markets because their cost structures and processes are tuned for high‑margin, high‑volume products.
- An organization’s processes and values are its capabilities; they become disabilities when the problem requires a different way of working.
- Technology supply often outpaces market demand, causing performance overshoot and a shift in the basis of competition.
- Successful response to disruption requires an autonomous unit with a cost structure matched to the new market.
- Discovery‑based planning assumes forecasts are wrong and focuses on learning rather than committing to uncertain projections.
- First‑mover advantage in disruptive markets belongs to entrants, not incumbents, because entrants are not constrained by legacy processes.
- Value networks shape what resources are allocated; changing the network can free a firm to pursue disruptive opportunities.

## Patterns

# Patterns

## Create Autonomous Unit for Disruptive Tech

**When to use:** When a new technology requires a different cost structure or market focus.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the disruptive technology and its target market.
2. Spin off a separate business unit with its own budget and decision‑making authority.
3. Design the unit’s processes and incentives around low‑margin, high‑growth objectives.
4. Protect the unit from incumbent resource‑allocation pressures.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## Discovery‑Based Planning Loop

**When to use:** When market size and demand are unknown.

**Recipe:**

1. Formulate hypotheses about customer needs and value propositions.
2. Allocate small, reversible investments to test each hypothesis.
3. Gather real‑world feedback and iterate the business model.
4. Scale only after validated learning reduces uncertainty.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## Match Team Size to Market Size

**When to use:** Large firms need to address a small, fast‑growing market.

**Recipe:**

1. Define the market’s current revenue potential.
2. Assemble a lean team whose total cost aligns with that potential.
3. Empower the team to make rapid decisions without senior‑level approval.
4. Review progress quarterly and adjust team size as the market grows.

**Chapter:** ch04

---

## Monitor Performance‑Demand Gap

**When to use:** To anticipate when the basis of competition will shift.

**Recipe:**

1. Track product performance metrics (e.g., speed, capacity) over time.
2. Measure customer willingness to pay for each performance level.
3. Identify when performance improvements exceed market willingness.
4. Develop lower‑margin offerings that capture the emerging price‑sensitive segment.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## Leverage First‑Mover Advantage in New Architecture

**When to use:** When a simple, off‑the‑shelf technology creates a new market.

**Recipe:**

1. Enter the market early with a minimal viable product.
2. Focus on the attributes valued by the new customers (size, price, power).
3. Iterate quickly based on early adopters’ feedback.
4. Scale production once the technology proves viable and demand grows.

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## Avoid Customer Capture Trap

**When to use:** Incumbents are pressured by existing customers to ignore low‑margin markets.

**Recipe:**

1. Map the influence of current customers on resource allocation.
2. Create a separate decision‑making channel for the new market.
3. Set performance metrics that reward progress in the emerging market, not just existing sales.
4. Periodically review and adjust incentives to keep the new channel independent.

**Chapter:** ch04

---

## Separate Cost Structures for Low‑Margin Business

**When to use:** When a disruptive product must be profitable at low margins.

**Recipe:**

1. Analyze the cost drivers of the incumbent’s high‑margin model.
2. Redesign processes to reduce fixed costs and increase variable cost efficiency.
3. Adopt lean manufacturing, modular design, and supply‑chain simplification.
4. Validate the new cost structure against target price points before scaling.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## Use Low‑End Entry Point to Open New Markets

**When to use:** When a disruptive technology cannot meet high‑end customer specs.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify a segment that values different attributes (e.g., size, convenience).
2. Design a product that meets those attributes at a lower price.
3. Launch in the low‑end market and use feedback to improve performance.
4. Gradually move upmarket as the technology matures.

**Chapter:** ch09

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## product

- If your biggest customers demand higher performance, ask whether a lower‑performance, lower‑price alternative could open a new market.
- If performance improvements are outpacing customer willingness to pay, start looking for price‑sensitive customers.
- When a disruptive technology appears, focus on the attributes valued by its early adopters, not on the attributes valued by your current customers.
- When a technology’s performance curve intersects the market demand curve, that is the moment to launch.

## general

- When a competitor enters at a lower price, assume they are right about the cost structure and you are wrong.
- Never let the voice of your most profitable customers drown out signals from emerging customers.

## process

- Never allocate capital based on a market forecast that you cannot verify.
- Match the size of the team to the current revenue potential of the market it serves.
- Treat every forecast as a hypothesis to be tested, not a fact.
- Monitor the gap between technology supply and market demand to predict shifts in competition.
- Use small, reversible bets to learn about a new market before committing large resources.
- When your cost structure is high‑margin, redesign processes to lower fixed costs before entering low‑margin markets.
- Track performance metrics separately for the disruptive unit to avoid contamination by legacy goals.
- If you cannot quantify the market, focus on learning the customer’s problem instead.

## organization

- Create a separate unit for any project that requires a different cost structure than your core business.
- If your organization’s values prioritize high margin, you cannot succeed in a low‑margin market without changing those values.
- Separate incentives for the new business from those of the legacy business.
- If your organization’s processes are rigid, create a new process team for the disruptive project.
- Allocate a dedicated budget for disruptive experiments; treat it as a sandbox, not a cost center.
- If your organization’s values reward speed over cost, you will struggle with low‑margin disruptive markets.

## design

- When a new architecture is technically simple, assume it is likely to be disruptive.
- When a new technology is simple and uses off‑the‑shelf parts, assume it is likely to be disruptive.

## strategy

- Avoid waiting for a market to become ‘big enough’; enter early with a minimal viable product.
- If incumbents are slow to adopt a new technology, look for entrants that can move faster.
- If a project threatens to cannibalize existing revenue, evaluate it on its own growth potential, not on short‑term loss.
