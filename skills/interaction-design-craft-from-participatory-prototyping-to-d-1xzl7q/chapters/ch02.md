# ch02 — Iconic and Spatial Metaphors

> The OGDEN white paper proposed using pictures of rooms, cabinets, and drawers to represent files—a spatial filing system that later influenced the desktop metaphor. Icons work because they remind users of familiar concepts, letting them grasp meaning at a glance.

In the early 1970s Tesler and Jeff Rulifson wrote OGDEN (Overly General Display Environment for Non-programmers), arguing that icons would be appropriate for computer interfaces because they act as signs that users can recognize without reading words. They sketched a 3‑D desk with a file cabinet, drawers, and a trash can, suggesting that users could point at semi‑realistic objects to issue commands. Although the diagram did not resemble today’s Mac or Windows, the idea caught Alan Kay’s attention and was passed to Dave Canfield Smith, who implemented the first recognizable icon set. The core insight is that interaction designers should look for real‑world analogues—folders, trash cans, desktops—that users already understand, then translate those into screen objects. This reduces the need for memorization and leverages existing perceptual skills, making the system feel immediate and intuitive.
