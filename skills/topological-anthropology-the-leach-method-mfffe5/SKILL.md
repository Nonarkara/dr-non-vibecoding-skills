## When to load this skill

A structural framework for analyzing social systems as sets of variables rather than fixed objects. This skill enables an agent to strip away cultural labels and biological assumptions to uncover the underlying mathematical patterns of kinship, power, and ritual influence.

## Core principles

- Societies are not naturally existing species but assemblages of variables.
- Structural patterns remain constant even when the manifest shape of a society changes.
- Kinship categories are sociological dogmas, not biological reflections.
- Social relationships are best understood as mathematical ratios (e.g., the ratio of maternal to paternal filiation).
- Marriage creates an enduring alliance between groups, not just a contract between individuals.
- Mystical influence serves as a symbolic marker for social alliance and political authority.
- Common substance dogmas (blood, bone, flesh) are used to define group incorporation.
- Universal definitions of social institutions are often sterile and obscure empirical reality.

## Topological Stretching

**When to use:** When comparing two societies that seem different but share a common logic.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the key social variables (e.g., father, mother, group).
2. Map the lines of connectedness between them.
3. Abstract the pattern into a geometric or algebraic form.
4. Compare this form to other societies to find structural constants.

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## The Zero-Filiation Extreme

**When to use:** When a society denies a biological link to one parent.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify which parent is excluded from 'kinship.'
2. Observe how the relationship with that parent is redefined as 'affinity.'
3. Look for economic or ritual payments that treat that parent as an outsider.
4. Analyze how this exclusion strengthens the boundaries of the remaining group.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## Substance-Symbol Mapping

**When to use:** When trying to understand group boundaries.

**Recipe:**

1. Ask what physical parts (bone, blood, flesh) are attributed to which parent.
2. Associate 'permanent' substances with group incorporation.
3. Associate 'temporary' or 'soft' substances with external alliance.
4. Use this map to predict inheritance and loyalty patterns.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## The Affinity-Authority Link

**When to use:** When analyzing political hierarchies in kinship-based societies.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the source of 'fate' or involuntary mystical influence.
2. Identify the source of 'sorcery' or intentional ritual attack.
3. If these two sources are the same affinal group, that group holds political authority.
4. Treat the marriage alliance as a tributary relationship.

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## Ritual Substitution

**When to use:** When a social role is filled by an unexpected person.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the expected role (e.g., husband).
2. Identify the substitute (e.g., son).
3. Analyze the logic of the substitution (e.g., son acting as affine to mother).
4. Use the substitution to uncover the underlying structural category.

**Chapter:** ch04

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## process

- Treat societies as variables, not things.
- Look for the ratio between parents, not just the presence of a bond.
- Watch for 'butterfly collecting' in your own analysis.
- Use topology to find constants in changing systems.

## design

- Ignore biological facts; focus on social dogmas.
- Identify who provides the 'bone' and who provides the 'flesh.'
- Remember that structural patterns can survive radical shape changes.
- Treat 'mother's husband' as a distinct category from 'father.'

## argument

- Use algebra to strip away value-loaded language.

## general

- Distinguish between involuntary fate and intentional magic.
- Check if siblings through one parent are considered unrelated.

## product

- Map supernatural threats to political hierarchies.
- Look for 'perpetual affinity' in political structures.

## writing

- Avoid universal definitions of 'marriage' or 'family.'
- Don't let English kinship terms bias your observations.
