# ch09 — Design for Safety and Stress

> Details how to shape products that keep users safe and effective when they are under high anxiety or time pressure.

Under negative affect, users focus narrowly on perceived threats and may miss obvious alternatives—a phenomenon known as tunnel vision. In dangerous contexts, designers must compensate by making critical information impossible to overlook and actions simple to execute under stress. This includes using high‑contrast alerts, auditory alarms that are unmistakable, and physical controls that require deliberate motion (e.g., a two‑handed safety switch). Feedback should be immediate and unambiguous, letting the user know whether an action succeeded or failed without needing to interpret subtle cues. Environments should reduce cognitive load: hide non‑essential controls, provide clear pathways, and use consistent labeling. Training and simulation can also help, but the design itself must not rely on the user remembering complex procedures under duress. For example, an aircraft cockpit places primary flight instruments directly in front of the pilot, with redundant alerts and tactile controls that can be operated even when vision is impaired. By shaping the product to support focused, accurate action even when the user is stressed, designers reduce the chance of error and increase confidence in high‑stakes situations.
