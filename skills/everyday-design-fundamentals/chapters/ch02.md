# ch02 — Signifiers and Communication

> Describes how signifiers convey meaning, both intentional (labels, icons) and accidental (traces, patterns), and why they are more important than affordances for discoverability.

Signifiers are any perceivable indicators that tell a user what to do. They can be deliberate, such as a "PUSH" label on a door, or accidental, like a worn path in snow that shows the preferred route. Signifiers translate the raw affordance into a clear instruction: a flat plate may afford pushing, but a raised arrow on the plate signals that pushing is the intended action. Good signifiers are visible, unambiguous, and placed close to the action they describe. When signifiers are missing or contradictory, users must guess, leading to trial‑and‑error and frustration. Designers should audit every interactive element for a signifier, ensuring that the cue matches the affordance and that the cue is perceivable under normal lighting and usage conditions. Accidental signifiers—such as a sticky note left on a button—can become misleading if they are not removed or replaced with intentional cues.
