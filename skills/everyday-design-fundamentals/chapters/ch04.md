# ch04 — Feedback: Timing and Content

> Explains why feedback must be prompt and informative, and how to avoid over‑ or under‑feedback that confuses users.

Feedback tells the user what happened after an action. It must arrive within a tenth of a second for simple actions; longer delays cause users to doubt whether the system responded. Feedback should also convey enough information to be useful. A single beep may confirm a button press but tells nothing about the state of the device. Richer feedback—such as a progress bar, a tone that varies with intensity, or a textual message—helps users understand the result and plan next steps. However, too much feedback can be distracting, especially in safety‑critical environments where alarms compete for attention. Designers should prioritize feedback: critical alerts get loud, persistent signals; routine confirmations get subtle cues. The feedback channel (visual, auditory, haptic) should match the user's focus; a visual cue is ineffective if the user’s eyes are elsewhere.
