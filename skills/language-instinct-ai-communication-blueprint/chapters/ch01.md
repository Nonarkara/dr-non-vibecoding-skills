# ch01 — The Instinctive Grammar Engine

> Explains why children learn grammar without teachers and how the brain builds a universal grammar from sparse data.

Human infants hear fragmented speech, yet by age three they produce sentences that obey complex rules they never heard explicitly. Experiments with children forming questions show they apply structure‑dependent transformations, moving the auxiliary that follows the subject phrase rather than the first auxiliary in the string. This behavior cannot be explained by simple imitation; it reveals an innate algorithm that groups words into hierarchical phrases and applies abstract operations. The algorithm, often called Universal Grammar, supplies a finite set of principles—such as the need for a subject, verb agreement, and phrase‑level movement—that generate an infinite number of sentences. Because the algorithm is hard‑wired, children can invent grammatical devices (e.g., the English -s suffix) even when their input lacks consistent examples. For an AI, this means that human listeners expect hidden structure; surface‑level word order is insufficient to guarantee comprehension.
