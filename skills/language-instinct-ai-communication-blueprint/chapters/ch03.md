# ch03 — Sign Languages and the Modality‑Independent Grammar

> Describes how visual‑manual languages obey the same grammatical principles as spoken languages, confirming the modality‑independence of the language instinct.

Deaf children raised with sign language acquire the same hierarchical structures as hearing children learn spoken language. ASL, for example, uses spatial verb inflection, topic marking, and subject‑verb agreement that mirror spoken morphology. When deaf children are deprived of a consistent sign system, they still invent a richer grammar than the input provides, as seen in the evolution from Nicaraguan LSN (a pidgin) to ISN (a creole). The brain’s language module therefore operates on abstract symbolic relations, not on acoustic features. AI agents should treat visual, auditory, or textual channels as interchangeable carriers of the same grammatical logic, and design messages that respect the underlying structure regardless of modality.
