## Core principles

- Good prose works like a window onto the world: the writer points the reader to something concrete that both can see.
- Write as if you are having a conversation; assume the reader is competent and will fill in gaps you do not spell out.
- Replace abstract nouns with vivid verbs and concrete details; a sentence about a thing should use a noun, a sentence about an action should use a verb.
- Avoid nominalizations (zombie nouns) because they hide the agent and make the sentence harder to picture.
- Use the active voice when you want the reader’s attention on the doer, and the passive only when the doer is irrelevant or distracting.
- Limit signposting and metadiscourse; let the flow of ideas guide the reader rather than a table of contents inside the paragraph.
- Reserve uncommon or fancy words for moments when they add precision or a memorable sound, not to show off.
- Hedging should be a deliberate choice, not a default habit; qualify statements only when the evidence truly warrants it.
- Clichés can be refreshed with fresh imagery or avoided altogether; stale idioms drain the reader’s visual brain.
- Sound matters: phonesthetic choices (words that feel right when spoken) reinforce meaning and keep the prose lively.

## Patterns

# Patterns

Reusable moves extracted from the source. Each is a recipe — context, then steps, then the chapter to read for the full argument.

## Show‑Then‑Explain

**When to use:** When introducing a new concept or fact.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify a concrete example that illustrates the idea.
2. Present the example first, letting the reader picture it.
3. Follow with a brief explanation that ties the example to the abstract concept.

**Chapter:** ch01

---

## Revive Zombie Nouns

**When to use:** When a sentence contains a nominalization that hides the agent.

**Recipe:**

1. Locate the noun ending in –tion, –ment, –ance, etc.
2. Find the original verb that produced the noun.
3. Rewrite the clause using the verb and a clear subject.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## Active‑Focus Choice

**When to use:** Deciding whether to use active or passive voice.

**Recipe:**

1. Ask: Who is the most important character for the reader to track?
2. If the doer matters, use active voice.
3. If the doer is irrelevant or distracting, switch to passive.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## Hedge‑Only‑When‑Needed

**When to use:** When a claim is uncertain or limited.

**Recipe:**

1. State the claim plainly.
2. Add a qualifier only if data or context truly limit the claim.
3. Explain the limitation in one short clause.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## Cliché‑Refresh

**When to use:** When a familiar idiom is unavoidable but feels stale.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the literal elements of the idiom.
2. Rewrite the sentence using those literal elements in the current context.
3. If possible, replace with a fresh metaphor that matches the concrete scene.

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## Phonesthetic Word Selection

**When to use:** Choosing an uncommon word for impact.

**Recipe:**

1. List candidate words that convey the precise meaning.
2. Read each aloud to feel its sound.
3. Select the word whose sound reinforces the intended image and is still understandable.

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## Minimal Signposting

**When to use:** When a paragraph transitions to a new idea.

**Recipe:**

1. Use a single transition word or question to cue the shift.
2. Avoid full‑sentence previews of the upcoming content.
3. Let the new sentence itself carry the forward momentum.

**Chapter:** ch04

---

## Concrete‑Verb Rule

**When to use:** Any sentence describing an action.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the action being described.
2. Choose a strong, specific verb for that action.
3. Replace any noun‑based description with the verb and a concrete object.

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## Avoid Unnecessary Metaconcepts

**When to use:** When academic jargon clouds meaning.

**Recipe:**

1. Spot abstract terms that package other ideas (e.g., “framework”, “level”).
2. Replace them with the concrete elements they refer to.
3. Rewrite the sentence using plain nouns and verbs.

**Chapter:** ch05

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## writing

- If a sentence is about a thing, use a noun.
- If a sentence is about what someone did, use a verb.
- When a sentence feels hard to parse, look for buried verbs hidden in nominalizations.
- Replace zombie nouns with their original verbs.
- Use active voice to keep the reader’s gaze on the doer.
- Use passive voice only to hide an irrelevant or distracting agent.
- Limit hedges to statements that truly need qualification.
- When you must hedge, add a brief explanation of the limitation.
- Swap stale clichés for fresh, literal descriptions.
- If you use an idiom, ensure its literal image matches your context.
- Choose words whose sound matches their meaning (phonesthetics).
- Reserve uncommon words for moments when they add precision or memorable sound.
- Avoid using more than two adjectives in a row; let the noun do the work.
- Never start a paragraph with “In this section we will…”, let the first sentence be the content.
- If a sentence contains a passive construction, ask whether the agent matters; if not, keep it passive.
- When describing a process, use verbs that convey motion (e.g., *drift*, *burst*, *collapse*).
- Replace abstract nouns with concrete verbs to restore agency.
- If a paragraph feels heavy, cut any metadiscourse that does not aid comprehension.
- Use first‑person pronouns when they clarify perspective; avoid them only when they add unnecessary distance.

## process

- Ask yourself: What does the reader need to know before this sentence?
- Show a concrete example before explaining an abstract idea.
- Keep signposts to a single transition word or question.
- Read your prose aloud; if it trips, rewrite for smoother rhythm.
- Before publishing, run the 10‑step classic‑style checklist.

## general

- When you think of a word, say it aloud; if it feels wrong, choose a different one.
