# ch08 — Limits, Conventions, and the Role of Interpretation

> Summarizes the circumstances under which measurement claims are underdetermined, the legitimate role of convention, and how to interpret numerical results when empirical structure is incomplete.

Measurement is never free of interpretation. Even with extensive quantities, the choice of unit and the idealization of objects (perfectly rigid rods, frictionless motion) involve conventions. For derived attributes, the choice of which fundamental variables to include in the product set reflects theoretical assumptions. In the social sciences, many constructs lack a clear concatenation operation, making extensive measurement impossible; researchers then rely on operational definitions or conventional scaling. Such conventions are legitimate when they are explicitly acknowledged and when the numbers are used consistently for prediction or comparison within a given framework. However, the AI should treat numbers derived from pure convention as ordinal at best unless there is independent evidence of the relevant empirical operations. Limits also arise from data imperfection: finite samples, measurement error, and theoretical idealizations mean that axioms can only be approximately satisfied. The proper response is to assess whether deviations are random and small enough to still support a representation theorem, or whether they systematically violate the conditions. When interpretation is unavoidable, the AI should make explicit what is being assumed (e.g., ‘we treat equal steps on the scale as equal intervals for the purpose of this model’) and remain open to revision if empirical evidence contradicts the assumption.
