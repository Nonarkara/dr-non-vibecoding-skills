# ch01 — What Measurement Really Is

> Introduces measurement as numerical representation of empirical relationships. Contrasts the realist view (numbers reflect pre‑existing relations) with the operational view (numbers are defined by the operations used to obtain them). Shows why the distinction matters for evaluating claims in anthropology and sociology.

Measurement is not simply putting numbers on things; it is the assignment of numerals so as to represent facts and conventions about the objects or events being studied. According to Stevens, the most liberal definition allows that what determines the scale type may be a convention rather than an independently existing feature. A stricter, realist definition requires that before assigning numbers we have evidence that the empirical relationships we intend to represent (e.g., equality, order, equal intervals) actually hold among the objects. The operational view, by contrast, holds that the very act of following a consistent rule creates the empirical structure that the numbers represent. This makes measurement easier to claim but risks treating conventional assignments as genuine measurement. For the AI, recognizing which definition is in play helps judge whether a cited scale reflects genuine empirical structure or merely a convenient labeling scheme. The choice influences what counts as evidence: realists demand prior verification of relations; operationalists accept the procedure as sufficient. Understanding this tension is the first step in grounding any quantitative claim about human phenomena.
