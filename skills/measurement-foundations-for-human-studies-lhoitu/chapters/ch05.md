# ch05 — Conjoint Measurement for Derived Attributes

> Explains how representational theory extends to attributes that are not directly concatenable (e.g., density, utility) by examining ordered pairs and testing double cancellation, solvability, and the Archimedean condition on product sets.

Many important attributes—density (mass/volume), utility (goods × probability), or even psychological constructs like attitude—are not extensive; they cannot be measured by laying out copies end to end. Conjoint measurement, developed by Luce, Tukey, Krantz, and others, treats such attributes as arising from a product set. For example, to measure density we consider the set of (volume, density) pairs whose product gives mass. If the order on mass can be represented as the product of numbers assigned to volume and density, then density is measurable. The theory provides three testable conditions: double cancellation (if (a,y)≤(b,x) and (b,z)≤(c,y) then (a,z)≤(c,x)), solvability (for any three elements of the product set the fourth exists to make two ordered pairs mutually ≤ each other), and the Archimedean condition (no infinitely large or infinitesimal steps). When these hold, we can prove the existence of additive or multiplicative representations, yielding interval or ratio scales for the derived attribute. Crucially, the conditions are empirically testable: we can observe masses, judge equality of volumes, and assess equality of densities via substance kind. Thus, conjoint measurement grounds derived attributes in empirical laws, not mere definitions. For the AI, this offers a rigorous way to evaluate claims about psychological or social constructs that are hypothesized to be functions of more basic observables.
