# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## design

- If you cannot see the user, go watch them in their natural setting.
- Keep a bug list: write down anything that frustrates or confuses a user.
- Set aside your expertise and try to see the world through the user’s eyes.
- Write a one‑sentence design brief that states the problem, user, and constraints.
- List every constraint you know, then ask how each could inspire a solution.
- Build the simplest prototype that can answer your next design question.
- Use cheap, fast materials (foam, clay, paper) for early prototypes.
- Test the prototype with real users and watch what they do, not just what they say.
- Keep prototypes in a shared space where anyone can pick them up and comment.
- Invite engineers to review design prototypes and designers to review engineering models.
- Use inexpensive, off‑the‑shelf parts when budget is tight to spark material creativity.
- Keep the original prototype and insight statements visible during later stages.
- When moving to production, check that the core user insight is still present in the design.

## process

- In brainstorming, defer judgment: no criticism of any idea, no matter how wild.
- Aim for quantity: set a goal for number of ideas, not for quality.
- Build on others’ ideas: use “yes, and…” to add to what someone just said.
- Encourage wild ideas: the wilder, the better, because they can lead to practical breakthroughs.
- Capture one idea per sticky note or card during the brainstorm burst.
- Group feedback into themes: works well, needs improvement, confusing.
- Hold a short stand‑up each day to share what you learned from interacting with prototypes.
- Treat time limits as a focusing tool: prioritize the most critical questions first.
- After each iteration, ask: What did we learn? What will we change next?
- Run a five‑day design sprint: observe, brainstorm, prototype, test, decide.

## decision

- If a prototype fails to meet a core need, pivot to a different concept.

## life

- Celebrate intelligent failures: share what you learned from what did not work.
