## Core principles

- Innovation begins with observing real people in real‑life situations to see what works, what confuses, what they hate, and what latent needs they cannot articulate.
- Effective brainstorming follows four rules: defer judgment, go for quantity, build on others’ ideas, and encourage wild ideas.
- A prototype is a question made physical; the goal is to learn, not to prove, so build the simplest thing that answers the next design question.
- Constraints such as time, budget, or materials should be embraced as fuel for creativity, not treated as obstacles.
- Iterate quickly: evaluate prototypes with users and the team, learn what works and what doesn’t, then improve in the next round.
- Cross‑functional collisions spark insight; put prototypes in shared spaces where engineers, designers, and users can interact.
- Hot teams are small, multidisciplinary groups given a clear goal and a tight deadline to create momentum and focus.
- Successful innovations are transferred to market by implementing the concept while preserving the creative intent that came before.
- A culture that tolerates failure and celebrates learning enables continuous innovation.
- Deep observation means watching behavior, not asking for opinions; users often cannot articulate what they need.

## Observation Safari

**When to use:** When you need to uncover unspoken user needs before generating ideas.

**Recipe:**

1. Define the user and setting to observe.
2. Go to the field with a notebook or device to record.
3. Watch for behaviors, workarounds, and points of confusion.
4. Note each observation as a bug list item or insight.
5. Debrief with the team to cluster observations into themes.

**Chapter:** ch01

---

## Brainstorm Blitz

**When to use:** When you need a large quantity of ideas quickly, following IDEO’s rules.

**Recipe:**

1. Post the four brainstorming rules where everyone can see them.
2. Present a clear problem statement and any relevant insights.
3. Set a timer for 20‑30 minutes and encourage rapid idea sharing.
4. Capture one idea per sticky note or card, no discussion during the burst.
5. After the timer, group similar ideas and vote on the most promising.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## Rapid Prototyping Loop

**When to use:** When you need to learn which aspects of a concept work before investing in refinement.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the next specific question you need to answer.
2. Choose the lowest fidelity that can answer that question (sketch, model, role‑play).
3. Build the prototype quickly, aiming for speed over polish.
4. Test it with users or internal experts and capture reactions.
5. Decide whether to iterate, pivot, or keep the concept based on what you learned.

**Chapter:** ch04

---

## Constraint Storm

**When to use:** When you feel stuck by limits and want to turn them into design prompts.

**Recipe:**

1. List all known constraints (time, budget, materials, regulations, etc.).
2. For each constraint, ask: How could this limitation inspire a solution?
3. Brainstorm ideas that use the constraint as a feature or advantage.
4. Select the most promising constraint‑driven concepts to prototype.
5. Prototype and test to see if the constraint‑based idea works in practice.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## Hot Team Formation

**When to use:** When you need focused, multidisciplinary effort on a tight deadline.

**Recipe:**

1. Pick a clear, single goal that can be achieved in one to two weeks.
2. Select 4‑6 members from different disciplines (design, engineering, human factors, business).
3. Give the team a dedicated space where prototypes can be displayed and handled.
4. Set a short, recurring check‑in (e.g., 15‑minute stand‑up) to share progress.
5. At the end, review outcomes and decide next steps based on what was learned.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## Prototype Wall Review

**When to use:** When you want spontaneous feedback from anyone passing by.

**Recipe:**

1. Choose a high‑traffic area and install shelves, tables, or walls for display.
2. Place the current prototypes where they can be picked up and handled.
3. Provide sticky notes and pens for passersby to leave comments.
4. Hold a brief stand‑up each day to review any new notes or observations.
5. Use the feedback to decide what to refine in the next iteration.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## Iteration Review

**When to use:** When you have tested a prototype and need to decide what to change next.

**Recipe:**

1. Gather all feedback from users, experts, and internal team members.
2. Sort feedback into themes: works well, needs improvement, confusing, missing.
3. Prioritize themes based on impact on core user needs and project goals.
4. Define one or two specific design questions for the next iteration.
5. Build the next prototype focused on answering those questions.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## Cross‑Functional Swap

**When to use:** When you want engineers and designers to see each other’s work early.

**Recipe:**

1. Invite engineers to a design review and designers to an engineering review.
2. Ask each group to spend 10‑15 minutes interacting with the other’s prototypes.
3. Capture observations on sticky notes: what surprised them, what they would change.
4. Discuss the notes together, looking for mismatches between intent and feasibility.
5. Agree on one or two concrete adjustments to make before the next prototype.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## Failure Postmortem

**When to use:** When a prototype or concept did not work and you want to learn from it.

**Recipe:**

1. Gather the team and revisit the original goal and hypotheses.
2. Describe what was tested, what happened, and what the data showed.
3. Identify which assumptions were invalidated by the result.
4. Extract one actionable lesson for future projects (e.g., a new observation method).
5. Record the lesson in a shared log so the team can refer back later.

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## Design Sprint Day Plan

**When to use:** When you need to run a five‑day, time‑boxed design sprint using IDEO steps.

**Recipe:**

1. Day 1: Understand – review brief, constraints, plan observation activities.
2. Day 2: Observe – go into the field, collect bug lists and insight statements.
3. Day 3: Brainstorm – run a timed session using the four rules, vote on top ideas.
4. Day 4: Prototype – build low‑fidelity models of the top ideas to answer specific questions.
5. Day 5: Test – show prototypes to target users, capture feedback, decide next steps.

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## design

- If you cannot see the user, go watch them in their natural setting.
- Keep a bug list: write down anything that frustrates or confuses a user.
- Set aside your expertise and try to see the world through the user’s eyes.
- Write a one‑sentence design brief that states the problem, user, and constraints.
- List every constraint you know, then ask how each could inspire a solution.
- Build the simplest prototype that can answer your next design question.
- Use cheap, fast materials (foam, clay, paper) for early prototypes.
- Test the prototype with real users and watch what they do, not just what they say.
- Keep prototypes in a shared space where anyone can pick them up and comment.
- Invite engineers to review design prototypes and designers to review engineering models.
- Use inexpensive, off‑the‑shelf parts when budget is tight to spark material creativity.
- Keep the original prototype and insight statements visible during later stages.
- When moving to production, check that the core user insight is still present in the design.

## process

- In brainstorming, defer judgment: no criticism of any idea, no matter how wild.
- Aim for quantity: set a goal for number of ideas, not for quality.
- Build on others’ ideas: use “yes, and…” to add to what someone just said.
- Encourage wild ideas: the wilder, the better, because they can lead to practical breakthroughs.
- Capture one idea per sticky note or card during the brainstorm burst.
- Group feedback into themes: works well, needs improvement, confusing.
- Hold a short stand‑up each day to share what you learned from interacting with prototypes.
- Treat time limits as a focusing tool: prioritize the most critical questions first.
- After each iteration, ask: What did we learn? What will we change next?
- Run a five‑day design sprint: observe, brainstorm, prototype, test, decide.

## decision

- If a prototype fails to meet a core need, pivot to a different concept.

## life

- Celebrate intelligent failures: share what you learned from what did not work.
