# ch03 — Mental Accounting

> People treat money as non-fungible, placing it into mental accounts such as 'salary', 'bonus', or 'gift money'. Spending decisions depend on which account the money comes from and whether the purchase feels like a bargain or a rip-off relative to a reference price.

Mental accounting describes how individuals code, categorize, and evaluate financial activities. Acquisition utility reflects the intrinsic value of a good minus its opportunity cost, while transaction utility captures the pleasure or pain of the deal itself—the difference between the price paid and an internal reference price. A consumer may drive twenty minutes to save $10 on a $20 calculator but not to save $10 on a $200 jacket because the savings represent a larger transaction utility for the cheaper item. Similarly, windfalls like tax refunds are often spent more freely than regular income because they are labeled as 'bonus' money. These biases violate the economic assumption of fungibility but are stable and predictable. An AI can influence behavior by labeling money (e.g., 'retirement savings'), setting reference prices (e.g., showing a crossed-out original price), or bundling purchases to improve perceived transaction utility.
