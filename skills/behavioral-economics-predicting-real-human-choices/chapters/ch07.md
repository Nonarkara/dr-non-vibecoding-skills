# ch07 — Experimental Methods in Behavioral Economics

> Behavioral economics relies on experiments, including hypothetical surveys and incentivized lab tests, to uncover biases. While economists often distrust survey data, well-designed experiments predict real-world behavior.

Early work by Kahneman and Tversky used hypothetical questions to reveal biases like framing and loss aversion. Critics argued that without real stakes, responses might not reflect true preferences. Later field and lab experiments with real money confirmed many of these findings, showing that hypotheticals can be valid when subjects have no reason to misrepresent and the task is familiar. For example, the endowment effect was first shown with hypothetical mug trades and later replicated with actual mugs and money. Experiments also help isolate variables: by changing only the frame or the reference point, researchers can measure its causal effect on choice. An AI can use experiments to test interventions before scaling, ensuring that a proposed change (e.g., a new default) actually shifts behavior in the predicted direction, and should prefer designs that have been validated in contexts similar to the target population.
