## When to load this skill

A systematic framework for designing products and services that customers actually want. It provides tools like the Value Proposition Canvas to align what you build with the specific jobs, pains, and gains of your target audience, moving from initial assumptions to validated market fit.

## Core principles

- Value is only created when your products address specific customer jobs, pains, or gains.
- The customer is the judge, jury, and executioner of your value proposition.
- Don't fall in love with your first idea; prototype multiple alternatives to find the best fit.
- Evidence from the market always beats opinions in the boardroom.
- Great value propositions focus on a few things done extremely well rather than many things done poorly.
- Fit is a continuous process of design and testing, not a one-time achievement.
- Understand the customer's context because priorities change based on where they are and who they are with.
- A great value proposition will fail without a scalable and profitable business model.

## Glossary

# Glossary

Terms and named ideas from the source, anchored to the chapter that explains them.

| Term | Definition | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Customer Job** | The tasks, problems, or needs a customer is trying to satisfy in their work or life. | ch02 |
| **Functional Job** | A specific task the customer wants to complete, like writing a report or eating healthy. | ch02 |
| **Social Job** | A job related to how a customer wants to be perceived by others, such as looking trendy or competent. | ch02 |
| **Emotional Job** | A job related to seeking a specific state of mind, such as feeling secure or at peace. | ch02 |
| **Pain** | Anything that annoys the customer before, during, or after a job, or prevents them from getting it done. | ch02 |
| **Gain** | The outcomes and benefits a customer wants to achieve, ranging from required to unexpected. | ch02 |
| **Pain Reliever** | A description of how a product or service eliminates or reduces a specific customer pain. | ch03 |
| **Gain Creator** | A description of how a product or service produces outcomes and benefits the customer values. | ch03 |
| **Problem-Solution Fit** | The stage where you have a plan that aligns your value map with customer needs on paper. | ch04 |
| **Product-Market Fit** | The stage where you have evidence that customers actually want and value your offer in the real world. | ch04 |
| **Business Model Fit** | The stage where your value proposition is part of a profitable and scalable business structure. | ch04 |
| **MVP** | Minimum Viable Product; the smallest set of features needed to test a value proposition with customers. | ch05 |
| **Napkin Sketch** | A very rough drawing used to quickly communicate and explore a core business idea. | ch05 |
| **Ad-lib** | A fill-in-the-blank sentence used to quickly define the essence of a value proposition. | ch05 |
| **Shrek Model** | An extreme or outrageous prototype designed to spark debate and test boundaries. | ch05 |
| **Technology Push** | Starting the design process with a new invention and looking for a problem it can solve. | ch06 |
| **Market Pull** | Starting the design process with a known customer problem and looking for a solution. | ch06 |
| **Economic Buyer** | The person in an organization who controls the budget and makes the actual purchase decision. | ch09 |
| **Saboteur** | A stakeholder who might try to block a purchase because it threatens their interests or status. | ch09 |
| **Workaround** | A makeshift solution a customer uses because no existing product solves their problem well. | ch08 |

## Patterns

# Patterns

Reusable moves extracted from the source. Each is a recipe — context, then steps, then the chapter to read for the full argument.

## The Razor Blade

**When to use:** When you want to create recurring revenue from a one-time product sale.

**Recipe:**

1. Sell a base product at a low price or loss
2. Create a required consumable component
3. Generate high-margin recurring revenue from the consumables

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## Servitization

**When to use:** When customers prefer outcomes over ownership.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify a product you currently sell
2. Bundle it with maintenance and support
3. Sell it as a subscription or pay-per-use service

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## The Low-Cost Disruptor

**When to use:** When a market is over-served with features customers don't want to pay for.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the core functional job
2. Strip away all non-essential features
3. Offer the basic solution at a significantly lower price

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## The Double-Sided Platform

**When to use:** When value is created by connecting two different groups.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify two interdependent customer segments (e.g., hosts and guests)
2. Design a value proposition for each side
3. Build a mechanism for them to interact

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## The Data Detective

**When to use:** When you need a foundation for customer research without spending money.

**Recipe:**

1. Search for secondary research reports
2. Analyze existing customer data and support logs
3. Look for patterns in competitor reviews

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## The Journalist

**When to use:** When you need to understand the 'why' behind customer behavior.

**Recipe:**

1. Create a list of open-ended questions about past behavior
2. Interview potential customers without pitching your idea
3. Look for emotional triggers and recurring pains

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## The Anthropologist

**When to use:** When customers can't articulate what they need.

**Recipe:**

1. Observe customers in their actual environment
2. Note where they struggle or use workarounds
3. Identify jobs they do that they don't mention in interviews

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## The Ad-Lib Pitch

**When to use:** When you need to clarify a vague idea quickly.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the product
2. Identify the target customer
3. Identify the job to be done
4. Identify the primary pain reliever or gain creator

**Chapter:** ch05

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## design

- If the customer doesn't care about a feature, it isn't a feature; it's waste.
- Focus on extreme pains first; moderate pains rarely drive sales.
- If a pain reliever doesn't map to a specific customer pain, delete it.
- Distinguish between 'Expected' gains and 'Unexpected' gains to find your edge.
- Target a few jobs, pains, and gains, but do so extremely well.

## process

- Ask 'why' five times to find the real job the customer is trying to do.
- Don't show your prototype too early; listen to the customer's problems first.
- In B2B, always identify the Saboteur before you try to close the deal.
- Keep prototypes cheap so they are easy to throw away when they fail.
- Measure pain severity in concrete units like minutes, dollars, or frequency.
- Don't mix several customer segments into one profile.

## general

- A value proposition is a description of benefits, not a marketing tagline.
- Context changes the job; a phone in a car is different from a phone in a meeting.
- Social and emotional jobs are often more important than functional ones.

## product

- Check for Business Model Fit before you spend money on scaling.
- Great value propositions are difficult to copy because they are embedded in a model.

## writing

- Use Ad-libs to clarify your value proposition in less than ten seconds.
- Never ask 'Would you buy this?'; ask 'How do you solve this today?'
