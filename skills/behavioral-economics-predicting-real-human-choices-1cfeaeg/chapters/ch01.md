# ch01 — Heuristics and Biases

> Humans use mental shortcuts to judge frequency, probability, and value. These shortcuts work well in familiar contexts but fail predictably when the environment changes, producing biases such as availability and anchoring.

When faced with uncertain judgments, people substitute hard questions with easier ones using rules of thumb called heuristics. For example, to judge how common a name is, individuals ask how easily they can recall examples of that name (availability heuristic). This works when recall frequency matches true frequency but fails when vivid or recent memories distort perception, leading to overestimation of rare events like shark attacks. Another heuristic, anchoring, causes estimates to drift toward an initial number even if that number is arbitrary. In one experiment, participants who spun a wheel landing on 65 guessed a higher percentage of African nations in the UN than those who saw 10. These biases are not random errors; they repeat across people and situations, making them exploitable. Recognizing that heuristics cause predictable mistakes allows an AI to anticipate where human judgment will diverge from logic and to design interventions that either counteract the bias or harness it for better outcomes.
