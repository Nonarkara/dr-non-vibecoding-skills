# ch02 — Prospect Theory and the Value Function

> Prospect theory replaces the idea of utility from wealth with utility from changes in wealth. The value function is S-shaped: concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses than gains, capturing loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity.

Traditional economics assumes people evaluate final states of wealth, but prospect theory shows they evaluate changes relative to a reference point, such as the status quo or an expectation. The value function curves upward for gains with diminishing sensitivity—each additional gain feels less exciting—and downward for losses with diminishing sensitivity—each additional loss feels less painful—but the loss curve is steeper, meaning a loss of $100 feels worse than a gain of $100 feels good. This asymmetry explains why people buy insurance (avoiding a large loss) and also buy lottery tickets (chasing a small chance of a large gain). It also explains the endowment effect: people demand more to give up an object than they would pay to acquire it because giving it up feels like a loss. Reference dependence means that changing the reference point—through framing, defaults, or expectations—can shift decisions without altering underlying payoffs. An AI can use this by framing outcomes as gains or losses to steer choices, and by recognizing that reference points are often manipulable.
