# ch06 — Extensive vs. Intensive Quantities in Human Studies

> Distinguishes attributes that admit concatenation (extensive) from those that do not (intensive, like temperature or pleasure) and shows how each can still be measured via different empirical routes.

Extensive quantities—length, mass, time—allow a physical concatenation operation: putting two rods end to end yields a rod whose length is the sum. This makes it possible to test axioms like associativity and the Archimedean condition directly with observable operations. Intensive quantities, by contrast, do not admit such a concatenation; temperature of two bodies mixed does not yield a temperature that is the sum. Nevertheless, intensive attributes can still be quantitative if they sustain ratios. Historical thinkers like Oresme argued that intensities can be imagined as lengths, thus inheriting the quantitative structure of extensive magnitudes. To measure an intensive attribute we must find a different empirical route: for temperature we rely on the expansion of mercury or the resistance of a wire, which themselves are extensive and whose changes correlate with temperature. The key is that the intensive attribute stands in a lawful, nomological relationship to an extensive one (e.g., pressure ∝ temperature × density). When the relationship is empirically confirmed, we can infer the intensive attribute’s quantitative structure from measurements of the extensive proxy. In psychology, many treats (e.g., mood, intelligence) are treated as intensive; the AI should check whether the proposed measure is linked via a confirmed law to an observable extensive proxy, or whether it relies solely on conventional scaling.
