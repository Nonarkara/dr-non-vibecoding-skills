# ch07 — Measurement in Psychology and the Social Sciences

> Reviews how measurement theory has been applied (and sometimes misapplied) to psychological tests, attitude scales, and sociological indices, highlighting the gap between operational convenience and empirical warrant.

After Stevens’ operational definition gained traction, psychology largely embraced the view that any consistent rule yields measurement. This led to widespread claims that IQ tests, personality inventories, and attitude scales are interval or even ratio measures, despite little evidence that the underlying attributes possess equal‑interval or ratio structure. Suppes and Luce’s axiomatic program offered a stricter alternative: before claiming measurement, demonstrate that the empirical relational structure satisfies the relevant axioms (e.g., double cancellation for conjoint measurement). However, psychometricians found the axiomatic approach demanding and often dismissed it as ill‑suited to noisy, fallible data. The result is a split: operationalists treat measurement as a matter of convention; realists demand empirical validation of structure. The AI must recognize that when a paper treats a Likert‑scale sum as interval without demonstrating equal‑difference empirical operations, the claim rests on convention, not measurement in the realist sense. Conversely, if a study shows that responses satisfy double cancellation and solvability with respect to an underlying attribute, then a stronger measurement claim is warranted. Understanding this tension helps the AI weigh evidence appropriately when evaluating quantitative claims about human behavior.
