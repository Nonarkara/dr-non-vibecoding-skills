## When to load this skill

This skill distills John Maeda's ten laws of simplicity into actionable chapters. Each law presents a concrete move—such as reducing features, organizing groups, saving time, or learning basics—that helps designers, engineers, and everyday creators make products and experiences feel simpler without stripping away meaning. By studying these laws, an agent can quickly pick the right tactic for a given simplicity challenge.

## Core principles

- Simplicity is achieved through thoughtful reduction, not just removal of features.
- Organization makes a large set of items appear fewer by grouping them.
- Saving time or making time feel shorter creates a sense of simplicity.
- Knowledge turns complex tasks into simple ones by making the familiar obvious.
- Simplicity and complexity depend on each other; one cannot exist without the other.
- What sits at the edge of a simple design influences its perceived simplicity.
- Designs that evoke more emotion feel simpler than those that feel flat.
- People trust simple designs when they behave predictably and reliably.
- Some inherent complexity cannot be eliminated; accepting limits is part of simplicity.
- The essence of simplicity is subtracting the obvious and adding the meaningful.

## Patterns

# Patterns

Reusable moves extracted from the source. Each is a recipe — context, then steps, then the chapter to read for the full argument.

## Apply thoughtful reduction via shrink‑hide‑embody

**When to use:** When you need to remove features but keep the product feeling valuable.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify removable functionality.
2. Shrink the remaining object to lower expectations.
3. Hide residual complexity behind simple interfaces.
4. Embody lost value with better quality or clear quality signals.

**Chapter:** ch01

---

## Use slip to organize a collection

**When to use:** When many items must feel manageable without discarding any.

**Recipe:**

1. Write each item on a note and move them to find natural clusters (sort).
2. Name or code each cluster (label).
3. Merge clusters that are truly alike (integrate).
4. Pull the vital few items into a top‑level set (prioritize).

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## Leverage a progress bar to embody time

**When to use:** When a process takes noticeable time and you want the wait to feel shorter.

**Recipe:**

1. Show a bar that fills steadily from start to finish.
2. Optionally add a numeric countdown of remaining time.
3. Ensure the bar moves smoothly, not in jerky jumps.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## Apply relate‑translate‑surprise to teach a concept

**When to use:** When introducing a new idea that could feel abstract or overwhelming.

**Recipe:**

1. Relate the idea to something the learner already knows.
2. Translate the relation into a tangible object, metaphor, or diagram.
3. Add an unexpected, pleasant detail that rewards the effort (surprise).

**Chapter:** ch04

---

## Balance simplicity and complexity as partners

**When to use:** When reducing features risks making the design feel bland or meaningless.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify which complexity gives the simple element its significance.
2. Retain that complexity in a subtle, supporting role.
3. Highlight the simple element by contrasting it against the retained complexity.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## Design the periphery to reinforce central simplicity

**When to use:** When a core element feels cluttered despite being simple.

**Recipe:**

1. Remove distractions from the background or surrounding area.
2. Use neutral colors, ample whitespace, or framing to guide the eye.
3. Ensure that borders, toolbars, or packaging do not compete for attention.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## Layer emotion onto a reduced form

**When to use:** When a simplified design feels cold or flat and needs warmth.

**Recipe:**

1. Choose an emotional tone (joy, comfort, curiosity) that fits the goal.
2. Add color, texture, sound, or micro‑interactions that evoke that tone.
3. Verify the emotional element supports, not obscures, the core function.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## Build trust through consistent, transparent behavior

**When to use:** When users hesitate to rely on a design because it feels unpredictable.

**Recipe:**

1. Make every interaction produce the same outcome each time.
2. Show progress or status so users know what is happening.
3. Admit limits or delays honestly rather than hiding them.

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## Design around unavoidable complexity

**When to use:** When a task truly requires multiple steps or decisions that cannot be removed.

**Recipe:**

1. Accept that some complexity will remain.
2. Break the task into clear stages with signposts.
3. Provide sensible defaults for the common case to reduce effort.

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## Subtract the obvious, add the meaningful

**When to use:** As a final check on any design to ensure it feels both lean and rich.

**Recipe:**

1. List elements that are obvious simply because they have always been there.
2. Remove those that do not add real user value.
3. Identify what users truly care about in this context.
4. Add back only those meaningful elements, even if they add a little complexity.

**Chapter:** ch10

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## design

- If a feature isn’t used, remove it.
- Make things smaller or lighter to lower user expectations.
- Hide complexity behind a simple interface that appears only when needed.
- Add real or perceived quality (better materials, clear signals) when you remove features.
- Sort items into piles, label each pile, integrate similar piles, prioritize the vital few.
- Use the tab key to turn a messy list into a clean table.
- Squint to see whether elements visually group into a whole.
- Remember that simplicity needs complexity to be meaningful; keep a contrasting element.
- Pay attention to the periphery: remove distractions, use whitespace, frame the center.
- Add warmth, joy, or curiosity through color, texture, sound, or micro‑interactions.
- Make every interaction behave the same way every time to build trust.
- Show progress or status honestly; admit limits rather than hiding failures.
- Accept that some complexity cannot be removed; design stages and defaults to manage it.
- Subtract the obvious elements, then add back only what is truly meaningful to the user.

## process

- Remove steps or replace choice with randomness to shrink time needed.
- Hide the passage of time by removing clocks or showing a smooth progress bar.
- Embody time with numerical countdowns, filling bars, or styling that suggests speed.

## general

- Learn by mastering the basics and repeating them often.
- Avoid overwhelming beginners with advanced features; start simple.
- Inspire learners with concrete examples of what they can achieve.
- Use relate‑translate‑surprise: connect to prior knowledge, show a mapping, add a surprise.
