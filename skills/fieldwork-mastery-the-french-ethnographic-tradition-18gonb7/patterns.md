# Patterns

Reusable moves extracted from the source. Each is a recipe — context, then steps, then the chapter to read for the full argument.

## The Defamiliarization Exercise

**When to use:** When you are too familiar with a research site to see its unique features.

**Recipe:**

1. Assume you are an explorer from a completely different world.
2. Pretend you do not understand the local language or social cues.
3. Document every physical movement and object as if it were a new discovery.
4. Record your own emotional and physical reactions to the environment.

## The Small-Scale Deep Dive

**When to use:** When you need to understand the total social logic of a group.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify a community of 200-300 people.
2. Spend enough time to learn every individual's name and role.
3. Map the relationships and conflicts between every member of the group.
4. Observe how technology and environment dictate their daily routines.

**Chapter:** ch04

## The Ritual Sequence Map

**When to use:** When analyzing a complex social ceremony or transition.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the moment of separation from the old status.
2. Document the 'liminal' phase: where are the participants and what are they doing?
3. Identify the moment of reincorporation into the new status.
4. Note the physical boundaries crossed during each phase.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## The Informal Narrative Prompt

**When to use:** When formal interviews are yielding superficial or rehearsed answers.

**Recipe:**

1. Abandon the structured questionnaire.
2. Ask open-ended questions about childhood or past events.
3. Allow the informant to digress and follow their own train of thought.
4. Listen for recurring themes or specific linguistic rhythms.

**Chapter:** ch10

## The Physical Participation Method

**When to use:** When trying to understand rituals or activities that involve altered states of mind.

**Recipe:**

1. Move beyond just watching and start doing.
2. Follow the physical movements of the participants exactly.
3. Pay attention to how the activity changes your own perception of time and space.
4. Record the 'internal illumination' that comes from direct experience.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## The Environmental Correlation

**When to use:** When trying to find the origin of a specific social hierarchy.

**Recipe:**

1. Study the biological history of the local plants and animals.
2. Observe how humans interact with these species (e.g., subduing vs. symbiosis).
3. Look for parallels between these interactions and the local social structure.
4. Determine if the religious or political hierarchy mirrors the ecological one.

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## The Value Identification Filter

**When to use:** When trying to understand the 'why' behind seemingly random customs.

**Recipe:**

1. Identify the primary binary oppositions used by the group (e.g., pure/impure).
2. Observe which side of the opposition is valued more highly.
3. Track how these values dictate social interactions and restrictions.
4. Determine if the society prioritizes the individual or the collective.

**Chapter:** ch09

---

## The Direct Contact Audit

**When to use:** When evaluating the quality of existing research or your own data.

**Recipe:**

1. Check if the researcher spoke the local language fluently.
2. Identify if the data came from intermediaries or direct observation.
3. Look for evidence of 'armchair' speculation vs. field-based facts.
4. Discard any claims that rely on 'knowing' the natives without questioning them.

**Chapter:** ch07
