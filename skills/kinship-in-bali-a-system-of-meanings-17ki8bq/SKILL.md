## When to load this skill

This skill provides a framework for analyzing social structures as overlapping networks of meaning rather than rigid hierarchies. It focuses on the Balinese model where kinship, residence, and irrigation are handled by distinct, autonomous groups, teaching agents how to analyze complex societies without projecting Western assumptions of unified community or biological kinship.

## Core principles

- Social organization is a collection of interpenetrating, functionally specific groups rather than a single unified community.
- Ritual and religion serve as the primary matrix for all public social relationships.
- Kinship is an organized system of meanings and corporate obligations, not just a biological fact.
- Political power and social prestige are distinct dimensions that do not always overlap.
- Decisions are reached through total consensus within autonomous groups to maintain social harmony.
- Membership in social groups is defined by specific obligations like work, ritual, or water rights rather than mere residence.
- The 'House' and the 'Kinship Group' are separate entities with different social functions.

## Functional Specificity

**When to use:** When a new social task or goal is identified.

**Recipe:**

1. Define the specific purpose of the group.
2. Recruit members based on their interest or relevance to that purpose.
3. Establish the group as an independent 'seka' with its own rules.
4. Ensure the group does not take on unrelated social functions.

**Chapter:** ch08

---

## Consensus Decision Making

**When to use:** When a corporate group needs to make a decision.

**Recipe:**

1. Convene a formal meeting of all members.
2. Discuss the proposal thoroughly in both formal and informal settings.
3. Seek a solution that everyone can agree to.
4. If any member strongly opposes, delay or abandon the proposal to maintain harmony.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## Cross-Cutting Membership

**When to use:** When designing a stable social system.

**Recipe:**

1. Create multiple autonomous groups for different functions (water, ritual, residence).
2. Allow individuals to hold memberships in many different groups simultaneously.
3. Ensure the boundaries of these groups do not perfectly overlap.
4. Use the resulting web of ties to prevent the formation of rigid, opposing factions.

**Chapter:** ch10

---

## Ritual as Political Foundation

**When to use:** When organizing a large-scale political or social entity.

**Recipe:**

1. Center the organization on a shared temple or ritual cycle.
2. Use ceremonies to mobilize labor and resources from the population.
3. Validate social rank through participation in and funding of dramatic displays.
4. Focus on the symbolic expression of order rather than administrative control.

**Chapter:** ch06

---

## The Way-Station Principle

**When to use:** When a central authority needs to maintain ties with distant groups.

**Recipe:**

1. Establish a 'way-station' temple or altar in the distant location.
2. Symbolize the presence of the central deity or authority at this branch.
3. Allow local members to fulfill their ritual duties at the branch to save travel.
4. Use the ritual link to maintain a sense of inclusion in the larger group.

**Chapter:** ch01

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## process

- If a group has a new task, form a new 'seka' rather than adding to an old one.
- Irrigation is managed by those who use the water, not those who live nearby.
- Use consensus to avoid the social friction of a majority-rule vote.
- If a hamlet grows too large, split it rather than centralizing power.

## design

- Distinguish between a person's inherited title and their actual political office.
- In a 'seka', all members are equal regardless of their status outside the group.
- Assume that social groups will overlap like an embroidered fabric, not a grid.

## general

- Look for the temple at the center of any organized Balinese group.
- Treat the hamlet as the site of secular life and the 'desa' as the site of sacred law.
- Identify the 'Origin' temple to find a group's historical identity.
- Separate the role of the temple priest from the high Brahmana priest.
- Look for the 'lontar' charter to understand a community's specific rules.
- Gentry ties are often regional while commoner ties are local.

## life

- Check if a woman's marriage aligns with her father's title rank.

## argument

- Recognize that a lord's power is often limited to ritual and war, not local law.
