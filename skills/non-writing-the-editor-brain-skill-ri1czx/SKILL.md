## Core principles

- Non writes alone, so every sentence must feel like a personal confession rather than a collaborative argument.
- An essay opens with a blunt claim, a vivid image, or a named thinker that instantly sets the tone.
- The body weaves personal experience into abstract ideas by letting the story run in a stream‑of‑consciousness flow before any theory is introduced.
- Closing moves are quiet aphorisms or open‑ended questions that leave the reader with a lingering thought.
- Signature techniques—short quotes, embedded dialogue, before/after contrast, and thinker‑load phrases—appear in every paragraph to create a recognizable rhythm.
- Do not use generic transitions; instead use the writer’s own voice to bridge ideas with a single, unexpected phrase.
- Never let the prose become a polished report; keep the raw, slightly messy feel that mirrors the writer’s thinking process.
- When a metaphor is introduced (e.g., biking, tunnels), it must be extended throughout the paragraph to anchor the argument.
- Every paragraph should end with a subtle pause—a comma, dash, or ellipsis—that mimics the writer’s breath.
- The voice thrives on self‑questioning; embed a rhetorical question in at least one sentence per section.

## Patterns

# Patterns

Reusable moves extracted from the source. Each is a recipe — context, then steps, then the chapter to read for the full argument.

## blunt_open

**When to use:** When you need immediate impact or a strong opinion statement.

**Recipe:**

1. Start with a direct claim about the topic.
2. Follow with a brief personal anecdote that illustrates the claim.
3. Insert a thinker reference to add weight.
4. End with a short pause or dash.

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## hook_then_turn

**When to use:** For narrative essays that begin with a vivid scene.

**Recipe:**

1. Describe a concrete image in one sentence.
2. Pivot with "so I thought" or similar phrase.
3. Introduce the personal relevance.
4. Tie back to the main argument using a thinker name.

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## stream_consciousness

**When to use:** When writing reflective journal entries or brainstorming drafts.

**Recipe:**

1. Set a timer (10‑15 minutes).
2. Write continuously without editing.
3. When the flow naturally pauses, note the emerging theme.
4. Insert the relevant theory or quote after the pause.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## before_after

**When to use:** To illustrate a change in perception or habit.

**Recipe:**

1. State the situation before the event.
2. Describe the event briefly.
3. State the situation after the event.
4. Conclude with a reflective line that links both states.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## thinker_load

**When to use:** When an essay needs an intellectual anchor.

**Recipe:**

1. Introduce the main idea.
2. Insert the name of a philosopher or author as a clause.
3. Explain how that thinker’s concept aligns with your experience.
4. Proceed with personal narrative.

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## quiet_aphorism_close

**When to use:** To end an essay with lingering thought.

**Recipe:**

1. Summarize the core insight in a single, pithy sentence.
2. Add a dash or ellipsis for a pause.
3. Optionally, pose an open‑ended question.

**Chapter:** ch04

---

## embedded_dialogue

**When to use:** When internal conflict needs dramatization.

**Recipe:**

1. Write a sentence describing a thought.
2. Insert a short quoted line of imagined speech.
3. Continue the narrative, showing the impact of that dialogue.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## contrast_reversal

**When to use:** To highlight tension between two opposing ideas.

**Recipe:**

1. State the first idea.
2. Introduce the opposite idea using "but" or "yet".
3. Explain how the two coexist.
4. Close with a reflective comment.

**Chapter:** ch07

---

## non_possessive_love

**When to use:** When discussing relationships without ownership language.

**Recipe:**

1. State the common possessive view.
2. Reject it with a clear statement of non‑possessiveness.
3. Provide a philosopher’s quote supporting the view.
4. End with a question about true love.

**Chapter:** ch08

## Cheatsheet

# Cheatsheet

Imperative decision rules from the source, grouped by category. Each is the kind of sentence you would write on an index card.

## writing

- Start with a blunt claim or vivid image; never begin with a generic statement.
- Insert a short quote or thinker name within the first paragraph.
- End with a quiet aphorism or an open‑ended question, not a summary.
- Never use generic transitions like "however"; replace them with personal asides.
- Add a short imagined dialogue to dramatize internal conflict.
- Never hide the writer’s voice behind neutral tone; keep the personal pronoun present.

## process

- Write a 10‑minute uninterrupted stream before adding any analysis.
- When describing a change, use the before/after pattern.
- Use the bike metaphor to illustrate personal transformation or focus.

## style

- Use dashes, ellipses, or commas to create pauses that mimic breathing.

## design

- Contrast two opposing ideas in the same paragraph to create tension.

## life

- Reject possessive language in love essays; frame love as mutual and non‑owning.

## general

- If the piece feels too polished, add a minor grammatical stumble to restore rawness.
- Close with a dash or ellipsis to leave the reader hanging.
- When in doubt, ask yourself: "Does this sentence sound like me talking to myself?"

## code

- When referencing a thinker, keep the citation brief—just the name and a short phrase.
