# ch01 — The Three Flavors of Incentives

> An introduction to economic, social, and moral incentives and how they work together to drive behavior.

An incentive is a means of urging people to do more of a good thing and less of a bad thing. They are not organic; they are invented by parents, politicians, or economists. There are three basic types: economic, social, and moral. An economic incentive involves a financial gain or loss, like a tax or a bonus. A social incentive involves how others perceive you, such as the shame of being seen doing something wrong. A moral incentive involves your own sense of right and wrong. Most systems use a mix of all three. For example, anti-smoking campaigns use high taxes (economic), bans in public places (social), and claims that tobacco money funds terrorism (moral). To understand any behavior, you must first map the web of incentives acting on the individual. If the behavior seems irrational, you have likely missed one of these three drivers. Incentives are the levers that move the world, but they are often small and hidden from plain view.
