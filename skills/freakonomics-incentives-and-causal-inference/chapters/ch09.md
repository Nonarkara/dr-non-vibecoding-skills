# ch09 — The Science of Causal Inference

> How to distinguish between things that happen at the same time and things that cause each other.

The most common mistake in reasoning is confusing correlation with causality. Just because two things happen together does not mean one caused the other. For instance, a political candidate who spends more money often wins, leading people to believe that money buys elections. But the data shows that a winning candidate can often decrease their spending and still win, while a losing candidate can double their spending and still lose. The money follows the candidate who is already likely to win; it doesn't create the win. To find true causality, economists use 'natural experiments'—situations where a random event or a policy change creates a control group. By comparing these groups, you can isolate the effect of a single factor. Without this rigor, you are just guessing based on patterns that might be purely coincidental. Always ask: 'If X changed but Y stayed the same, would the outcome be different?'
