## Core principles

- Writing is a solitary act of learning where thinking happens between the idea and the fingers.
- The best depository of knowledge is the human head, not the notebook.
- Reflection is a performance multiplier that codifies experience into wisdom.
- Start every piece with the single most powerful story you can remember.
- Physical movement like biking or running decompresses the mind and allows the true self to surface.
- Write for the sake of writing to avoid the self-censorship caused by audience anxiety.
- Challenge every tradition or custom that lacks a reasonable, logical foundation.
- The goal of writing is to emerge from self-imposed immaturity through reason.

## The Memory-First Opening

**When to use:** When starting a new essay or chapter and feeling overwhelmed by data.

**Recipe:**

1. Put away all notes, books, and references.
2. Identify the single most powerful image or event you remember from the experience.
3. Write that story as if you are telling it to a friend over a drink.
4. Let the story dictate the structure of the rest of the piece.

**Chapter:** ch02

---

## The 15-Minute Codification

**When to use:** At the end of a workday to ensure lessons are not lost.

**Recipe:**

1. Set a timer for 15 minutes.
2. Write down the main takeaways and successful stories of the day.
3. Identify one negative emotion or failure and describe it clearly.
4. Save these notes as a 'floor map' for the next day's work.

**Chapter:** ch03

---

## Physical Decompression

**When to use:** When stuck on a complex problem or feeling mentally 'closed-in'.

**Recipe:**

1. Engage in a repetitive physical activity like biking or walking.
2. Focus entirely on the immediate environment (the road, the traffic).
3. Allow the mind to drift into imaginary scenarios or parallel universes.
4. Keep a recorder or notepad ready for the 'true self' ideas that surface.

**Chapter:** ch05

---

## The Philosophical Pivot

**When to use:** When writing about a mundane observation and wanting to add depth.

**Recipe:**

1. Describe a specific, everyday scene (e.g., people in a coffee shop).
2. Ask a 'why' question about the underlying custom or behavior.
3. Connect that behavior to a known philosophical concept (e.g., Kant's immaturity or Hume's skepticism).
4. Argue for a new way of seeing the mundane through that philosophical lens.

**Chapter:** ch08

## process

- If you don't know what to write, write to find out.
- Look at the floor of the tunnel (daily steps), not just the walls (final goal).
- Reflection is the difference between a path and a random walk.

## writing

- Write until you physically drop to capture a true stream of consciousness.
- Censor the audience, not your own ideas.
- A powerful story is a better anchor than a perfect theory.
- Treat writing as a friend who helps you think twice.
- The best writing happens in the gap between the idea and the fingers.

- Never follow a tradition that you cannot defend with reason.

## life
