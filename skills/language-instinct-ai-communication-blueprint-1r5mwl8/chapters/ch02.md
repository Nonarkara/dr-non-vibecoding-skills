# ch02 — From Pidgin to Creole: Children as Language Engineers

> Shows how children transform rudimentary communication systems into fully fledged languages, illustrating the creative power of the language instinct.

When speakers of different tongues are forced to cooperate, they often develop a pidgin: a stripped‑down lexical mix with minimal grammar. Historical cases—from Pacific plantation labor to Nicaraguan deaf communities—demonstrate that once children are exposed to a pidgin as their primary input, they enrich it with systematic morphology, word order, and inflection. The resulting creole displays the same grammatical categories (tense, aspect, agreement) found in older languages, despite never having seen them. This rapid, single‑generation invention proves that the language faculty does more than copy; it actively generates rules to fill gaps. For AI design, the lesson is clear: when a human audience lacks a shared formal system, the AI should provide minimal scaffolding (consistent markers, repeatable patterns) and let the audience’s own language engine fill in the rest.
