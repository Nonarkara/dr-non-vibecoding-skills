# ch04 — Credibility

> Explains how to make ideas believable through authority, anti‑authority, vivid details, and the Sinatra Test, so the audience trusts the message.

Credibility convinces the audience that the idea is worth believing. There are two main sources: external credibility (authority figures, anti‑authority figures with relevant experience) and internal credibility (details that let the audience test the idea for themselves). An authority source, such as a doctor recommending a vaccine, leverages trust in expertise. An anti‑authority source, like a former smoker warning about lung cancer, can be more persuasive because the speaker has lived the experience. Vivid details also boost credibility; a concrete, specific example feels more trustworthy than a vague statistic. The Sinatra Test asks: if you can make it here, you can make it anywhere—if one outstanding example demonstrates the idea, it stands for the whole. For instance, a security system that protects Fort Knox is credible for protecting any bank. Statistics gain credibility when they are presented in a human‑scale format, such as “one out of every three children” instead of “33%.” Ultimately, credibility lets the audience lower their guard and consider the message on its merits.
