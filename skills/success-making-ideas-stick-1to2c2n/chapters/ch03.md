# ch03 — Concreteness

> Describes how to make ideas concrete by using sensory language, specific nouns, and vivid details that the audience can picture and remember.

Concrete language grounds ideas in the real world, making them easier to understand and recall. Abstract concepts like “justice” or “efficiency” are vague; concrete phrases such as “a woman handing a loaf of bread to a hungry child” create a mental image. To make an idea concrete, replace abstractions with sensory details—what you can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell. For instance, instead of saying “improve customer satisfaction,” describe “a customer smiling as they receive their order two minutes early.” The brain stores concrete information as vivid images, which are more durable than abstract symbols. Using concrete nouns also helps people apply the idea: a guideline like “keep the floor dry” is clearer than “maintain safety standards.” The Pomelo example shows concreteness through analogy: describing a pomelo as a “supersized grapefruit” taps into a familiar schema, making the new concept instantly graspable. Concreteness also builds credibility, because specific details signal truthfulness.
